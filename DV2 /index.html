Robotics in Australia

Four industries, four different paths

Since 1994, Australia's workforce has not changed evenly and the reason Technology, it shows in every industry but in different ways.

Overview

The workforce has not changed evenly

Health care has more than tripled its jobs while mining has nearly tripled its own. Manufacturing and agriculture now employ about a fifth fewer people than they did and in the pages ahead, we will look at how technology is working for each industry, from factory floors and mine sites to paddocks and hospital wards.

1 · Mining

Automation moves to where the ore is

Mining was one of the first industries to put automation to work at scale, its sites are remote, its machines are enormous and the work is repetitive. Driverless haul trucks and autonomous drills now operate across the Pilbara and other mining regions increasing output by about 180% since the mid 1990s however, multifactor productivity has fallen by about 32% meaning more is being dug up but at the cost of greater inputs.

2 · Manufacturing

Fewer workers, more output per hour

Manufacturing is the industry most people picture when they think of robots replacing workers, but the data tells a quieter story. Since 1994-95 the industry has lost about 14% of its hours worked, yet each hour is producing about 34% more, so the gain has come from productivity rather than from a sudden wave of job losses. Multifactor productivity has risen only about 8%, a little behind the wider market sector at 12%, which suggests the increased productivity is owed to better use of labour as to new machines. Manufacturing story Manufacturing now produces about 16% more than it did in 1994-95 while commiting 14% fewer hours which is where the 34% gain in output per hour appears. For workers, that means the industry needs fewer people for each unit of output and its share of all jobs has fallen from about 13% to under 6%. The technology behind this is not mainly robots, manufacturers invest more than other businesses in older digital tools such as electronic data exchange (15.3% against 9.5%) and in 3D printing (5.8% against 2.1%) but lack in artificial intelligence (9.3% against 12.1%) and data analytics (2.6% against 5.2%). Manufacturing is also one of the more innovation-active industries, with 55.4% of businesses innovating against 45.6% across all businesses.

4 · Health care

Robots in the operating theatre

Health care is where Australia’s workforce has grown the most with its share of all jobs rising from about 8.5% to 16% since 1994. Technology here looks very different from a mine or a factory, as the robots work alongside surgeons in the operating theatre rather than replacing a shift of workers. Robotic procedures on the da Vinci system in Australia and New Zealand grew from 11,496 in 2018 to 19,989 in 2022, an increase of about 74%. Access and cost For a man having a prostate removed, robotic surgery in the Victorian study meant going home after about a day and a half instead of four or five with none of the robotic patients requiring a blood transfusion. That comes at a cost though, with each robotic case adding thousands of dollars to the bill and a single da Vinci robot costing around A$3.9 million to buy. It is no surprise then that only 26 of the 162 robots in Australia and New Zealand are in public hospitals and just 14 of 153 in Australia are in rural or remote areas. Whether you get the better recovery depends a lot on where you live and whether you can pay for private care.
5 · The workplace

What changed for the people doing the work

Technology is only one thing that has changed about work since 1994, the hours put in and what they earn has shifted very differently from one industry to the next. Mining still has the longest working weeks and the highest income per person, though its lead over the average has narrowed, health care, which has grown the most sits below the average income with some of the shortest working weeks. Agriculture now sits on average with the national income aswell as manufacturing. The chart below follows each industry from 1994-95 to 2025-26, so you can see which way each one has moved.

Workplace takeaway: Farm workers have gained the most relative to the average with health care workers slipping farther behind. Income per person in agriculture rose from 62% to 99% with weekly hours unchanged at about 35. Health care fell from 100% to 87% of the average at about 26 hours a week, while mining workers still earn about twice the average 207%, down from 229% and work the longest weeks, rising from 40 to 42 hours. Manufacturing hours fell from 35.4 to 33.0 a week, with income steady at about the average.

Conclusion

Summary: The innovation of technology has had four very different effects on Australian work. Mining automation sits alongside a huge increase in output, yet the industry now uses more resources to get each tonne out. Manufacturing is steady and efficient, with about 14% fewer hours producing about 16% more outputs. Agriculture productivity has almost tripled with slightly fewer hours with farm incomes having caught up to the national average, and in health care, robots work beside surgeons with patients recovering faster, but at the cost of higher bills and limited access. What links these is that technology rarely acts alone weather, prices, skills, policy and who can afford the machines, all shape what it delivers, and the data here shows association rather than proof that technology caused the change. Robotics in Australia is not a single story the benefits reach some workers, regions and patients well before others.
